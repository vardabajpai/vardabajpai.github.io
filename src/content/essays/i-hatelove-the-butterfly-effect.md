<p>I started this in my notes app September of last year and got about a paragraph in before I abandoned it. It's hard to tell now what prompted the abruption-- there are about a thousand reasons I could think of, and there are a thousand things that have changed within and around me since that time. It could've been a phone call from one of the ~twenty people who probably (hopefully) don't have my contact saved anymore. More likely, it could've been my inattentive and frustrating tendency to wander in my mind faster than I can type: a trait that I have not been able to shake in the last year. The first paragraph, however unfinished, still resonated with the slightly older version of me; I guess some things don't really change. In hopes that you (my imaginary room of attentive empty chairs) don't develop jet lag from the sudden change in time zone, I am going to pick it up from here and attempt to call on only the elements and thoughts that seem to be innate for me, cemented in my psyche and unwilling to change even if everything else does.</p><div><hr></div><p>I have a very hard time with serious conversations. That’s actually a bit less than true— I suppose if I was really averse to them, I wouldn’t find myself caught in long discussions filled with harshly honest advice as frequently as I do. Nevertheless, in my mind, I hate the idea that any aspect of our lives must be dissected and reframed in pseudo-therapy speak for the purpose of reorienting the emotions and actions of the people around us. I often catch myself wishing I could ghost off the planet and live a completely inconsequential life; then I remember the price of solitude is loneliness, and the risk of collision is rewarded by connection.</p><p></p><p>There is a popular concept that I used to dislike that I would like to introduce now. In simple terms, the butterfly effect is the idea that a flap of a butterfly’s wings could lead to cascading effects that trigger larger changes, eventually causing a tornado miles away. Pseudo-scientifically, every breath a human takes creates a larger atmospheric disturbance than a butterfly flapping its wings. All of this to say, if the butterfly effect is taken to be truth, the moment that a human is born,</p><p>--</p><p>it sets into motion an inevitably cataclysmic series of reactions beyond our control or comprehension. This, of course, is immediately terrifying to think of. Instinctively, I hate it and would like to write off the entire concept immediately. How can it be true that even if I were to sit in my bedroom for the entirety of my life, just the simple act of breathing could have untold and unforeseen impacts? Of course, I am well aware that this whole scenario is largely hyperbolic. But, for a second, I will let myself believe it, and imagine the entire world as a pond in the rain; every breath taken acting as a raindrop causing rippling, cascading interactions that make up time and effect everything. A tree falls in front of my house and blocks me in because a girl in India cried when she fell playing cricket. California wildfires trace back to inhales and exhales of a shared cigarette on a balcony in Amsterdam. Everything and everyone is constantly interacting and colliding in ways that we have absolutely no control over.</p><p></p><p>When I was 16, I got into a car accident that was partially my fault. There is only one person in the world who I was entirely and completely vulnerable with in the two hours following the accident, and we don't speak anymore. Even that brief shock-induced hysterical vulnerability covered about a fifth of what I felt in the following weeks, and what I still feel today when I think about it. This will not be entirely unpacked here because like I said, I don't like serious conversations. I will, however, say this-- sitting behind a thousand-pound weapon, in a streaming flow of similar weapons, seeing in front of you the impact that your simple miscalculation had on the lives of complete strangers? It makes you want to sit in your bedroom for the rest of your life out of sheer fear of the power you have that you are entirely unequipped to hold. I never wanted to drive a car again. I never wanted to even get on a bicycle again. I, at 16, was already operating in a constant state of fear of hurting those around me emotionally, and now I was rudely awakened to the possibility that I could cause physical harm without intention as well. Now, compound this with the thought that even if I were to sit in my bedroom for the rest of my life, my breath alone somehow had the power to knock over a village in Somalia, and then write a script that somehow all of this harm, regardless of intention, is entirely my fault.</p><p></p><p>Hate it. Hate all of it. Cancel the idea itself.</p><p></p><p>Over time, however, I've come to realize that there may have been a few holes in my thinking, possibly dug by the 16-year-old tendency to hate everything, wallow in self-misery, and hide from the world forever. More importantly, likely caused by the teenage tendency to dip your feet in concrete and center yourself entirely in the way you see the world.</p><p></p><p>The first step on my path of reconciliation with the butterfly effect was realizing that everything is not horrible. If a breath can topple a village, it has equal right to land the humanitarian relief team weeks later. If a breath can make you cry, it could also make you laugh. The same vehicle that may cause harm can also drive strangers to the hospital-- and intent (bolstered by wisdom) actually gives you a lot more autonomy over your impact in those situations. Not every cascade is cataclysmic-- some can be collective, and positively impactful, and maybe beautiful.</p><p></p><p>Next up was realizing that hiding does nothing. The tree will fall anyways-- if not that one, then perhaps the one next to it. Yes, you have the power to cause unforeseen, cripplingly terrifying change in the world. So what? So does everyone. There are eight billion people on the planet, and countless other creatures we share our home with, and everything is breathing, and the world will change no matter what. So what can we do?</p><p></p><p>The answer to this question, as it's developed in my mind over time, is not "nothing." Stubbornly, "nothing" never even occurred to me. My mental pendulum swung straight from "disappear so at least you have no part in it" right over to where I've landed now: play bumper cars with the entire world. Yes, there is nothing we can do about the things we can't do anything about, but that leaves a whole universe for us to play in. And if we are stuck impacting everything whether we like it or not, why not like it? Why not spend our lives colliding entirely on purpose?</p><p></p><p>The thing about connecting with people is that you leave so much room for disaster. The closer you get with the people around you, the more power you have to hurt them, and vice versa. But if the butterfly effect('s strange, long-winded interpretation) stipulates that everyone will get hurt anyways, your proximity to life only really dictates the impact that your intent can have. You can choose to get really close to your universe, to stick your head in the water knowing that you will feel the ripples of every raindrop, but the rain actually almost never feels as harsh when you're already swimming. Even if you try to pretend that staying far away will mean that you can't hurt anyone, your breath guarantees that isn't the case. The only alternative left, then, is to get close with good intentions. Hope, pray, work hard, and fail but then learn how to make sure the impact lands correctly. It feels a lot more okay to break things you can fix when you understand the impact out of your control. Yes, you can fuck up and make your friends angry, and it will suck but you will learn, and you can always remember that it's a lot more manageable than the poor village that is being toppled and receiving aid just because we are all alive.</p><p></p><p>Yes, you will hurt people in your lifetime. You will also get hurt. But when you let that be okay, you realize just how much power you have to help, and be kind, and leave ripples in the direction of your choosing. Now that you're here, you don't get to decide to impact nothing, so you have every incentive to play bumper cars with the world and collide intentionally.</p><p></p><p>The butterfly effect tells me that we exist in a constant state of collision with things and people that we don't even know about, and there is absolutely nothing that can be done about it. Really, there was no point in even writing it down, besides to point at the pond and say, "Wow. Look at that. That is beautiful."</p>
